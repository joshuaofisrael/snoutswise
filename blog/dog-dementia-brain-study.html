News: senior dogs with dementia show early Alzheimer's-like brain changes

Published 18 October 2026 · Last reviewed 18 October 2026 · By the SnoutsWise team

A study published in npj Dementia on 6 October 2026 examined the brains of 24 senior pet dogs donated by their owners. The dogs had amyloid-beta deposits similar to the early stages of Alzheimer's disease in people, and dogs rated as cognitively impaired had more of it. Age was the strongest predictor of how much amyloid built up. The researchers say aging pet dogs could help scientists study the earliest stages of Alzheimer's.

What the researchers did

Abdullatif Alsulami, Julie Moreno, Caitlin Latimer and colleagues, working with the Dog Aging Project, examined post-mortem brain tissue from 24 client-owned senior dogs. According to UW Medicine, the analyses were carried out at Colorado State University and UW Medicine in Seattle. The team adapted the staging methods pathologists use for human Alzheimer's disease and compared the brain findings with each dog's earlier score on the Canine Cognitive Dysfunction Rating, a questionnaire about behaviour, and with blood biomarkers.

What they found

  • The senior dogs had amyloid-beta deposits in a pattern consistent with early regional involvement in the human staging system.
  • Some had phosphorylated tau, another protein linked to Alzheimer's, but it was variable and restricted to certain regions. No neuritic plaques were found.
  • Cognitively impaired dogs had a greater amyloid burden, and age was the strongest predictor of amyloid build-up.
  • Blood levels of a protein called GFAP rose with amyloid plaque burden. The other blood markers tested did not track it.

In the UW Medicine release, Latimer said no one had mapped this pathology in dogs before and that the dogs could represent a model of the earliest stages of Alzheimer's disease. Moreno said the work supports companion dogs as an important model for studying Alzheimer's and related diseases.

What it means for owners

  • Canine cognitive dysfunction is real. UW Medicine notes it is typically seen in dogs over 8 years old, with signs such as disorientation, changes in social interaction and a decline in everyday function.
  • Tell your vet about changes in an older dog. Getting lost in familiar places, changed sleep, new house soiling or less interest in family can have many causes, including pain or other illness, so a check-up comes first (see dog health basics).
  • This is not a test or a treatment yet. The study was small and looked at brains after death. Blood markers such as GFAP need more research before they could help in the clinic.
  • Big dogs age faster. Use our dog age calculator and read about size and DNA aging to know when your dog counts as a senior.

Frequently asked questions

Can dogs get Alzheimer's disease?

Dogs get canine cognitive dysfunction, a form of dementia. A 2026 npj Dementia study found senior dogs develop early Alzheimer's-like amyloid deposits, with more in cognitively impaired dogs.

What are signs of dementia in dogs?

Signs include disorientation, changes in social interaction and a decline in everyday function, typically in dogs over about 8 years old. See your vet, because pain and other illnesses can cause similar changes.

Is there a blood test for dog dementia?

Not yet in routine use. In the 2026 study, blood GFAP levels rose with amyloid plaque burden, but the other markers tested did not, and more research is needed.
